# Create from source material

Use this workflow to transform documents, reports, notes, and research into a coherent presentation narrative. The source is the factual authority, but its page order and page layout are not presentation requirements.

The objective is not to summarize each page. Build a story that helps the intended audience understand the source's most important ideas, the evidence behind them, and their implications without changing the source's meaning.

## Shared workflow

Follow this sequence before and during generation:

```text
SOURCE
  → Understand
  → Extract
  → Normalize
  → Prioritize
  → Construct narrative
  → Assign slide purposes
  → Choose visual representations
  → Generate
  → Quality check
```

Do not skip directly from reading the source to writing slides. The extraction, normalization, and planning stages are necessary to preserve facts while improving the narrative.

## 1. Understand the source

Read enough of the source to understand it as a whole before deciding on slide order.

Identify:

- the document's subject, scope, and time period;
- its apparent purpose and intended reader;
- the user's stated audience, presentation purpose, and constraints, when provided;
- the central thesis, conclusions, or decisions;
- the major sections and how they relate;
- the terminology, definitions, measurement conventions, and qualifications used;
- whether appendices, footnotes, captions, or methodology sections affect the interpretation of headline claims; and
- areas where the source is incomplete, ambiguous, internally inconsistent, or explicitly uncertain.

When the audience or purpose is provided, use it to decide emphasis, explanation depth, and sequence. Do not use it to alter facts. When it is not provided, infer only what the source reasonably supports and avoid inventing a specific audience need.

Do not treat headings, page breaks, or visual prominence in the source as proof of narrative importance. A qualification in a footnote may be essential; a full source page may contribute only one supporting detail.

## 2. Extract ideas and evidence

Create a structured inventory of presentation-relevant material. Extract concepts before drafting slide copy.

For each candidate item, record:

- the idea, claim, finding, or question;
- the evidence that supports it;
- whether the evidence is quantitative, qualitative, or both;
- its source location, such as page, section, table, figure, note, or paragraph;
- its units, dates, periods, population, geography, scope, and definitions;
- qualifications, uncertainty, exceptions, limitations, or dependencies;
- its relationship to other ideas; and
- whether it is central, supporting, contextual, or appendix-level.

Treat tables, charts, footnotes, and captions as content rather than decoration. Capture the provenance of important figures and claims at extraction time; reconstructing provenance after slides are written is less reliable.

Do not copy every sentence into the inventory. Extract the meaning and retain the exact wording only when wording itself matters, such as a quotation, formal definition, legal statement, or carefully qualified claim.

## 3. Normalize without changing meaning

Normalize the inventory so related material can be compared and organized safely.

- Standardize labels for the same entity, period, segment, or measure while retaining the source's original terminology where it carries meaning.
- Group duplicate or overlapping claims and keep the strongest available supporting evidence.
- Link a headline result to the definition, denominator, baseline, or methodology needed to interpret it.
- Keep units, signs, date ranges, reporting periods, currencies, and scale factors explicit.
- Keep distinct metrics separate when their definitions differ.
- Mark reported, adjusted, organic, constant-currency, estimated, forecast, and comparable figures exactly as the source distinguishes them.
- Mark uncertainty, ranges, assumptions, exclusions, and data limitations.
- Record conflicts rather than silently resolving them. If the source itself reconciles the difference, preserve that reconciliation.

Normalization may improve consistency of presentation, but it must never manufacture comparability that the source does not establish.

## 4. Prioritize the material

Do not treat every fact as equally important. Rank candidate ideas according to their contribution to the presentation's purpose.

An idea is more likely to deserve prominence when it:

- is necessary to understand the thesis or conclusion;
- answers a likely audience question;
- changes how the audience should interpret the situation;
- provides decisive evidence;
- explains a driver, constraint, risk, or implication;
- establishes essential context or a definition; or
- prevents a headline claim from becoming misleading.

Classify material as one of:

- **Core:** necessary to the main narrative;
- **Supporting:** useful evidence or explanation that strengthens a core idea;
- **Optional depth:** valuable detail suited to progressive disclosure or an appendix; or
- **Out of scope:** unrelated to the presentation purpose and safe to omit.

Omission must not distort the remaining content. Retain qualifications and context that materially affect interpretation even when they make the story less concise.

## 5. Evaluate each important idea

Before assigning an idea to a slide, answer:

1. What is the main message?
2. Why does the audience need to know it?
3. What evidence supports it?
4. Is the evidence quantitative, qualitative, or mixed?
5. What is the simplest visual form that communicates it accurately?
6. Does it deserve a full slide, belong with a related idea, or fit better in supporting detail?
7. Would progressive disclosure improve the first reading without hiding essential meaning?
8. Does the claim, figure, or quotation require a citation?

If the main message cannot be stated clearly, the idea is not ready to become a slide. Clarify its role or combine it with related material before proceeding.

## 6. Construct the narrative

Arrange the prioritized ideas into a logical argument rather than preserving source order automatically.

A useful general structure is:

```text
Context → Key insight → Evidence → Implication → Action or conclusion
```

Use it only when it fits the material. Other valid structures include:

- situation → problem → solution → evidence → implications;
- strategy → execution → results → outlook;
- market → customer need → solution → differentiation → opportunity; and
- performance → drivers → constraints → outlook.

These are examples, not templates. Scientific findings, research reviews, educational material, case studies, and technical explanations may require different structures.

Build the sequence by checking:

- What must the audience understand first?
- Which question does each slide answer?
- What new understanding permits the next slide to make sense?
- Where does evidence belong closest to the claim it supports?
- Where are transitions needed to mark a change in topic, time, scale, or argument?
- What belongs in the main sequence, optional detail, or appendix?

When appropriate, the presentation should have a recognizable beginning, middle, and end:

- The beginning establishes the subject, stakes, and governing question or thesis.
- The middle develops the reasoning and evidence in a comprehensible sequence.
- The end resolves the governing question, states implications, or identifies the supported next step.

Do not manufacture a dramatic arc when the source supports only a neutral explanation or reference presentation.

## 7. Assign slide purposes

Give every planned slide one primary communication goal and a purpose that describes its job in the narrative.

Possible purposes include:

- opening or thesis;
- context;
- problem;
- opportunity;
- strategic idea;
- evidence;
- big number;
- comparison;
- trend;
- timeline;
- process;
- framework;
- relationship;
- segment or category;
- geographic view;
- case study;
- financial performance;
- risk or limitation;
- outlook;
- conclusion; and
- appendix or supporting detail.

This vocabulary is not exhaustive. Choose the purpose the material needs, and do not force every presentation into the same sequence.

### Decide whether to combine or split

Combine ideas when they:

- answer the same audience question;
- use the same evidence to support one conclusion;
- form a direct comparison; or
- are individually too minor to justify separate narrative steps.

Split ideas when they:

- compete for primary emphasis;
- require different evidence or visual forms;
- contain several reasoning steps that cannot be scanned comfortably;
- need separate qualifications or citations to remain accurate; or
- would make the slide unreadable at an appropriate type size.

Do not force every piece of information onto a slide merely because it came from the same source section.

## 8. Control content density

When a planned slide contains too much information, resolve it in this order:

1. Improve grouping, hierarchy, labels, and reading order.
2. Remove genuine repetition without removing distinct evidence or qualifications.
3. Move useful secondary detail into progressive disclosure when the primary message remains complete without interaction.
4. Split the content into multiple slides when each resulting slide has a meaningful purpose.
5. Move nonessential depth to an appendix when it should remain available but is not part of the main argument.

Never solve overcrowding by shrinking typography excessively. Do not turn every paragraph into bullet points; use concise prose, annotation, comparison, or another structure that matches the information.

## 9. Choose the visual representation

Choose the simplest form that makes the intended relationship easy to understand. The presence of numbers does not by itself justify a chart.

Common mappings include:

| Information need | Suitable representation |
| --- | --- |
| One important verified value | Big number with unit, period, context, and source |
| Change over time | Line or bar chart with an accurate time axis |
| Ranking | Ordered bars or a clearly ordered list |
| Direct comparison | Aligned comparison layout |
| Chronological events | Timeline |
| Ordered steps | Process |
| Structural or causal relationship | Diagram |
| Categories or segments | Structured grouping |
| Exact values across several dimensions | Table |
| Qualitative argument | Editorial text |
| Source wording that matters | Attributed quote |
| Primary idea plus optional depth | Progressive disclosure |

Before choosing a chart, ask whether the audience needs to see a pattern, change, distribution, or comparison. If the message is a single value, state the value. If exact lookup matters more than shape, use a table. If the source does not provide enough data to construct a truthful visual, use text or another supported form.

Do not create decorative diagrams that imply relationships absent from the source. Do not create icons, images, or visual metaphors that could be mistaken for evidence.

## 10. Preserve source integrity

For financial, scientific, technical, legal, medical, policy, or otherwise sensitive material, preserve all distinctions that materially affect meaning.

Keep explicit:

- units, currencies, scale, and signs;
- dates, periods, baselines, and comparison windows;
- populations, geographies, segments, and sample definitions;
- definitions and calculation methods;
- qualifiers, exceptions, exclusions, and assumptions;
- ranges, uncertainty, confidence, and limitations;
- actual, estimated, projected, and scenario values;
- reported, adjusted, organic, and constant-currency measures;
- GAAP and non-GAAP measures; and
- source attribution.

Never silently merge metrics with different definitions. Never convert correlation into causation, a projection into a result, a range into a point estimate, or a qualified statement into an absolute claim.

When simplifying copy, compare the result with the source and confirm that the shortened wording preserves scope, certainty, direction, and magnitude.

## 11. Maintain citations and provenance

Important claims, statistics, financial figures, quotations, and externally attributed statements should retain source information whenever it is available.

- Prefer the most precise stable provenance the source supports: page, section, table, figure, note, or appendix.
- Associate each citation with the specific claim or evidence it supports.
- Preserve author, organization, document title, publication date, and link when provided and relevant.
- Distinguish the supplied source from sources merely mentioned inside it.
- Keep citations with extracted items and carry them into the slide plan.
- Do not invent a page number, URL, author, date, or citation when the source does not provide one.
- If provenance is incomplete, record what is known and mark the gap rather than guessing.

Plan provenance so a later HTML implementation can present it visibly or through an accessible interaction. The primary claim must remain understandable without opening the citation detail.

## 12. Produce the slide plan

Before generating HTML, create an internal slide plan. It is a planning artifact and need not be shown to the user unless requested.

For every slide, record:

| Field | Required planning content |
| --- | --- |
| Slide number | Provisional position in the narrative |
| Slide purpose | The slide's job in the story |
| Primary message | The one idea the audience should retain |
| Supporting evidence | Claims, data, examples, or qualifications needed |
| Proposed visual form | The simplest accurate representation |
| Source citations | Traceable provenance for relevant claims and evidence |
| Optional interaction | Useful secondary detail or exploration, if justified |

Also note transitions, section boundaries, and appendix placement when they affect comprehension.

A planned slide is not ready when its primary message is only a topic label, its evidence is untraceable, or its visual form does not match the information relationship.

## 13. Generate from the approved plan

Use the slide plan as the factual and narrative specification for generation. The generated presentation may refine wording, layout, and interaction, but it must not introduce unsupported claims or change the planned logic without repeating the relevant integrity checks.

Keep source evidence connected to the slide it supports. If generation reveals that a slide is overcrowded, return to the density sequence rather than removing qualifications or reducing text to an unreadable size.

## Pre-generation quality check

Before generation continues, verify that:

- the narrative has a clear beginning, middle, and end when the material calls for one;
- the ordering follows audience understanding rather than source pagination;
- each slide has one primary communication goal;
- core ideas, evidence, qualifications, and limitations remain accurate;
- no missing information has been invented;
- no omission changes the source's meaning;
- the amount of detail is appropriate to each slide's purpose;
- related information is combined and overloaded information is split appropriately;
- every visual assignment matches the relationship in the evidence;
- charts are used only when they communicate more clearly than a number, text, or table;
- important claims and figures can be traced to their source;
- metric definitions and distinctions remain intact; and
- the planned presentation does not simply reproduce the source document's original order or layout.

If any check fails, revise the extraction, narrative, slide purpose, or visual assignment before generating HTML.
