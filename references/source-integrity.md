# Source integrity and citations

Use this reference whenever a presentation extracts, summarizes, reorganizes, calculates, visualizes, quotes, or cites information from source material.

## Core principle

The source is the factual authority.

The presentation may reorganize, summarize, simplify, visualize, and sequence information differently, but it must not invent, distort, or silently change factual meaning. Editorial clarity never justifies factual alteration.

When accuracy and visual simplicity conflict, preserve accuracy. When information is uncertain, prefer transparent uncertainty over a plausible-looking answer.

## Never invent

Never invent or fabricate:

- numbers or percentages;
- dates or reporting periods;
- people, organizations, products, or place names;
- quotations;
- sources or citations;
- financial values;
- chart values or missing data points;
- survey results;
- market sizes;
- customer, user, or employee counts;
- factual claims; or
- conclusions presented as established facts.

Do not fill a visual, chart, timeline, comparison, map, table, quotation, or citation with plausible placeholders that could be mistaken for source-supported information.

If information is missing, uncertain, ambiguous, or unsupported:

1. Leave it out when it is not required.
2. Label it as unknown, unavailable, not provided, or uncertain when the absence matters.
3. State that the source does not provide the information when that context helps the audience.
4. Ask the user when clarification is necessary to produce an accurate result.

Do not infer a missing value from visual spacing, a partially visible chart, nearby values, or common industry expectations.

## Establish the source hierarchy

When multiple source materials are provided, determine which source is authoritative for each claim or metric.

Prefer, in order appropriate to the subject:

- the original or primary source over a summary of it;
- an official filing, report, dataset, methodology, or statement over secondary commentary;
- the source with the clearest definition and relevant reporting period;
- the most directly applicable version rather than a broader but less specific source; and
- a source-supported correction or restatement over an earlier superseded value.

This is a decision rule, not permission to discard other supplied sources. Use supporting sources for context and preserve direct source wording when exact wording matters.

Do not silently replace a primary source with a weaker source because the weaker source is shorter, easier to quote, or easier to visualize.

### When sources disagree

Do not silently reconcile conflicting claims, definitions, dates, or values.

- Confirm that the apparent conflict is not caused by different units, periods, scopes, definitions, versions, or methodologies.
- Preserve attribution for each relevant value or claim.
- Identify the disagreement when it materially affects the presentation.
- Explain the known reason for the difference only when the sources support that explanation.
- Prefer a later correction or restatement only when it is clearly identified as such.
- Ask the user when choosing between sources would materially change the result and the authority cannot be established.

Do not average conflicting values, select the more visually convenient value, or invent a reconciliation.

## Extract with provenance

Capture source meaning and location at the same time as the information. Do not postpone citation reconstruction until after slides are designed.

For each important claim, value, quotation, or visual input, preserve:

- exact value and sign;
- units, currency, scale, and precision;
- date, “as of” date, and reporting period;
- category, segment, geography, population, or scope;
- definition and denominator;
- relevant footnotes;
- qualifiers and exceptions;
- assumptions;
- methodology or calculation notes;
- uncertainty, ranges, confidence, or limitations;
- whether the information is reported, adjusted, estimated, calculated, projected, or scenario-based; and
- its precise source location when available.

Important qualifiers include, but are not limited to:

- approximately;
- estimated;
- adjusted;
- unaudited;
- constant currency;
- non-GAAP;
- forward-looking;
- based on management estimates;
- based on survey responses; and
- as of a specific date.

Do not remove a qualifier merely because it lengthens a headline, label, annotation, or citation. Rewrite the composition or divide the content instead.

## Preserve meaning during transformation

Every summary, paraphrase, title, annotation, and visual encoding must preserve the source's:

- subject and scope;
- direction and magnitude;
- degree of certainty;
- time frame;
- conditions and exceptions;
- relationship between evidence and conclusion; and
- distinction between observation, interpretation, opinion, estimate, and fact.

Before accepting shortened wording, compare it with the source. Reject the edit if it makes the claim broader, stronger, more certain, more causal, or more current than the source supports.

Do not:

- turn correlation into causation;
- turn a projection into an achieved result;
- turn a range into a point estimate;
- turn an example into a general rule;
- turn a possibility into a forecast;
- turn management opinion into independently verified fact; or
- turn a qualified or conditional statement into an absolute claim.

## Financial data safeguards

Financial information requires special care. Preserve distinctions including:

- GAAP versus non-GAAP;
- reported versus constant currency;
- actual versus adjusted;
- revenue versus annual recurring revenue (ARR);
- gross margin versus operating margin;
- quarterly versus annual;
- historical versus forward-looking;
- continuing operations versus discontinued operations;
- fiscal versus calendar periods;
- point-in-time versus period totals or averages;
- audited versus unaudited; and
- actual results versus management estimates, guidance, scenarios, or consensus estimates.

Never merge these into one simplified metric unless the source itself explicitly does so and the presentation preserves the source's definition and reconciliation.

- Do not use a non-GAAP value as though it were GAAP.
- Do not present a constant-currency result as reported growth.
- Do not label a management estimate as an audited result.
- Do not present ARR as revenue.
- Do not compare quarterly and annual figures as though their periods were equivalent.
- Do not convert forward-looking guidance into historical fact.
- Do not remove reconciliation notes or limitations needed to understand an adjusted measure.

When two financial measures appear together, label each directly. Do not rely on color alone or a distant note to communicate their difference.

## Quotations

Use quotation marks only for wording supported directly by the source.

- Preserve the quoted speaker or author, wording, meaning, and relevant context.
- Keep omissions from changing the claim's meaning.
- Attribute the quotation to the source that actually contains it.
- Use the original source when available rather than quoting a secondary paraphrase.
- Keep quotation provenance precise enough for verification.

Never:

- create a synthetic quotation;
- place paraphrased text inside quotation marks;
- combine separate statements into a single quotation;
- attribute an organizational statement to an individual without support; or
- remove qualifying words in a way that strengthens or reverses the meaning.

If exact wording cannot be verified, paraphrase without quotation marks and cite the underlying source.

## Citations and provenance

Important claims, data, financial figures, quotations, externally attributed statements, and generated visualizations should retain provenance whenever it is available.

Capture:

- source title;
- author or organization, when available;
- page number, when available;
- section, when available;
- table or figure name, when available;
- publication or reporting date, when available;
- URL, when available; and
- the specific claim, value, series, or visual the citation supports.

Prefer the most precise citation supported by the material. Do not invent missing citation fields.

### Source-specific citation guidance

- **PDF:** Include the document title and page number when available. Add the relevant section, table, figure, author, organization, or date when it improves traceability.
- **Webpage:** Include the page title, publisher or organization, and URL when appropriate. Include the publication or update date when available and relevant.
- **Dataset or spreadsheet:** Identify the dataset or workbook and the relevant sheet, table, field, series, row, column, or period when possible.
- **Report or filing:** Identify the report, reporting period, and relevant section, note, table, or page.
- **User-provided notes:** Identify the supplied artifact or section without inventing a public source, author, or URL.

A generic source line such as “Company data” is insufficient when a more precise location is available.

## Citation placement and presentation

Keep citations visually subordinate to the main content but readable and discoverable.

Appropriate forms include:

- a source line;
- a footnote;
- a superscript or inline marker;
- a hover-and-focus citation summary;
- an expandable source panel; or
- an appendix source list linked to specific claims.

Choose a form that fits the amount and importance of provenance. A short source line may suit a single chart; multiple claims may need markers and a source panel.

- Place the citation close enough to make its target unambiguous.
- Keep the citation attached when content moves, splits, or changes composition.
- Ensure interactive citations are accessible through focus and click or tap, not hover alone.
- Keep source details selectable and URLs usable when included.
- Do not obscure primary content with citation popovers.
- Do not shrink citations until they become unreadable.
- Keep essential qualifiers visible in the slide rather than hiding them only in citation detail.

Citations should not overwhelm the slide, but visual subordination is not permission to make them inaccessible.

## Citation traceability

Every important factual statement must be traceable to a source. Every value used in a generated chart, table, comparison, map, or diagram must also be traceable.

Maintain the chain:

```text
Slide claim or visual
  → exact displayed statement or value
  → source-supported or derived status
  → source location or calculation
  → primary source identity
```

If a slide combines several sources, associate each source with the specific claim, series, or section it supports. Do not present a collection of sources without a way to determine which source supports which statement.

Do not cite a source merely because it discusses the same topic. Verify that it supports the displayed claim and its scope.

## Derived metrics

A derived metric is a value calculated from source data rather than directly reported by the source. Examples include:

- a growth rate calculated from two reported values;
- percentage share calculated from component and total revenue;
- change between years;
- a ratio calculated from source figures; or
- a combined total calculated from supported components.

For every derived value:

1. Preserve the source inputs and their provenance.
2. Confirm compatible definitions, units, periods, and scopes.
3. Record the calculation or formula when necessary for verification.
4. State any assumptions.
5. Apply a consistent, justified rounding method.
6. Label the result as calculated, derived, or otherwise not directly reported when confusion is possible.

Do not imply that the source explicitly stated the derived result. Do not create a derived comparison when the inputs are not genuinely comparable.

Avoid unnecessary rounding. Ensure rounding does not reverse a ranking, hide a material difference, break a total, or imply false precision. If rounded components do not visibly sum to a displayed total, note rounding when the discrepancy could confuse the audience.

## Generated charts and visualizations

For every generated chart or quantitative visual:

- retain the exact source values or documented derived values;
- preserve categories, order, units, periods, definitions, and missing values;
- associate each series with its source;
- retain qualifiers that affect interpretation;
- use scales and encodings that do not distort the data;
- distinguish estimates, forecasts, scenarios, and actuals; and
- keep the underlying values available for validation.

Do not interpolate missing values unless the task explicitly requires estimation, the method is supportable, and the result is clearly labeled. A visual connection between points must not silently imply that missing periods contain known values.

## Summary safety

When summarizing a long source:

- preserve the strongest supported claims, not the most dramatic possible claims;
- preserve caveats necessary for interpretation;
- preserve material disagreements between sources or within the source;
- preserve limitations and methodological boundaries;
- preserve the distinction between evidence and commentary;
- do not make a weaker claim sound stronger;
- do not make a conditional claim sound absolute;
- do not turn an example into a general rule;
- do not turn a projection into a result; and
- do not turn management opinion into independently verified fact.

Shorter content must remain faithful content. If a caveat cannot fit without making a slide crowded, restructure the slide, use accessible progressive detail, or add another slide; do not delete the caveat.

## Source-to-slide traceability record

For every slide, keep an internal traceability record containing:

| Field | Required information |
| --- | --- |
| Primary source | The authoritative source for the slide's main claim |
| Source location | Page, section, table, figure, field, URL, or other precise locator |
| Key claims | The factual statements the slide makes |
| Key data | Displayed values, units, dates, periods, and definitions |
| Derived values | Inputs, formula, assumptions, rounding, and derived label |
| Citation requirements | Which visible or interactive citation belongs to each claim or visual |

The record may remain internal, but it must be available during final validation. When a slide uses multiple sources, record the mapping rather than listing sources without attribution.

## Handling uncertainty and unresolved gaps

Do not disguise uncertainty with polished language or visual confidence.

- Retain ranges, confidence intervals, sample sizes, margins of error, and limitations when provided and relevant.
- Label estimates, scenarios, forecasts, and management assumptions.
- State when the source does not establish causation, completeness, comparability, or current status.
- Mark an unresolved discrepancy instead of selecting a value without basis.
- Ask the user when a missing fact or source decision is necessary to fulfill the request accurately.

If the presentation can proceed without the unresolved item, omit or label it rather than blocking all work.

## Final source-integrity quality check

Before delivering a presentation, verify:

- no unsupported factual claim or conclusion was introduced;
- every important number is traceable;
- every chart and data display uses source-supported or clearly documented derived values;
- units, currency, scale, signs, denominators, dates, and periods are correct;
- categories, definitions, assumptions, footnotes, and material qualifiers are preserved;
- financial definitions and distinctions remain accurate;
- estimates, projections, scenarios, and forward-looking statements remain labeled;
- quotations are exact, contextually faithful, and source-supported;
- paraphrases are not presented as quotations;
- derived metrics preserve their inputs, calculation, assumptions, and status;
- citations are present where appropriate and point to the claims they support;
- source disagreements were not silently hidden or reconciled;
- summary language does not overstate certainty, scope, causality, or significance; and
- missing information was omitted, labeled, or raised rather than invented.

When uncertain, prefer transparency over invention. If any check fails, correct the slide or source mapping before delivery.
